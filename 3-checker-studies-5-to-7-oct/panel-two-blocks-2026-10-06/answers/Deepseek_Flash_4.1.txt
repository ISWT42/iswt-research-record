Below is a review, not a publication — I only read what's public. Where I could not confirm something, I've marked it **[unsure]**.

Two notes before the answers. First, your two building blocks are usually called (1) an append-only, hash-chained log and (2) a commitment scheme with a reveal phase. Second, the closest existing work to your current use is not one paper but a small cluster: the belief-ledger design note [ai.bluecloudcyber.com](https://ai.bluecloudcyber.com/notes/belief-ledger), the `evidence.*` family in an IETF draft [datatracker.ietf.org](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/draft-msebenzi-evidence-action-00), Acta [github.com](https://github.com/VeritasActa/acta) / [veritasacta.com](https://veritasacta.com/docs), and "proof packets" [harperz9.github.io](https://harperz9.github.io/papers/proof-packets.pdf). Read those before claiming novelty.

## 1. Other uses, ranked

1. **Sealed, pre-registered evaluations of AI systems.** Regulators, model developers, procurement teams and independent eval labs need to prove that the test items, the scoring rule and the model identifier were fixed *before* the run and haven't moved since. It would replace self-reported model cards and benchmark numbers that can't be tied to a frozen test or a frozen model — the contamination and model-swapping problems. This is my pick for the most valuable new use, because it is the case where both building blocks are load-bearing rather than decorative.
2. **Pre-registered research, analyses and forecasts.** Scientists, journals, forecasters and investigative reporters need to show that a hypothesis, analysis plan or prediction was fixed before the data arrived. It would replace OSF/AsPredicted/ClinicalTrials.gov registration and Registered Reports, which today rest on trust and institutional sanction rather than cryptographic proof ([cos.io](https://www.cos.io/initiatives/prereg), [clinicaltrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov)).
3. **Software and model supply-chain provenance.** CI/CD systems, downstream consumers and regulators need to show an artifact was built from a specific commit by a specific builder, with no entries removed from the log. It would replace signed checksums plus manual attestations, and it is already partly occupied by Sigstore/Rekor and SLSA ([sigstore.dev](https://www.sigstore.dev), [slsa.dev](https://slsa.dev)) — so the value here is incremental, not new.
4. **Continuous audit and proof of reserves.** Auditors, exchanges and financial regulators need a liabilities snapshot and a reserves snapshot committed at a time and not edited afterwards. It would replace quarterly sampling and management letters — sampling proves nothing about the moments you didn't look.
5. **Sealed-bid procurement and auctions.** Governments, corporate buyers and auction houses need to show bids were fixed before the deadline and opened only after it. It would replace two-envelope systems and trusted auctioneers, with the caveat that the auctioneer's own record is still the thing being trusted.
6. **Whistleblowing, journalism and evidence preservation.** Sources, reporters and courts need to show a document existed, unaltered, at a date — without revealing it yet. It would replace lawyers' escrow and notaries, and it directly inherits the permanence problem (see §3).
7. **Secret custody and threshold access.** Crypto custodians, estates and executors, disaster recovery and multi-party key holders need to show that no single party could reconstruct a secret and that every reconstruction attempt is on the record. It would replace single-custodian escrow and "break glass" procedures; the mathematics already exists as verifiable secret sharing ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verifiable_secret_sharing)).
8. **Creative and IP provenance / prior art.** Creators, inventors and platforms need to show they had a work at a time and that it hasn't changed. It would replace registered mail, notarised drafts and platform timestamps; C2PA is the nearest deployed standard ([c2pa.org](https://c2pa.org)).

Also plausible but weaker: insurance and warranty claims, tenancy and construction photo records, emissions and ESG reporting, and governance balloting (weaker because a voter receipt enables coercion, which is why real e-voting schemes deliberately make receipts unusable).

## 2. What already exists

**Append-only and transparency logs.** Merkle hash chains date to Haber & Stornetta (1991) [link.springer.com](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00196791); Certificate Transparency is the canonical deployment ([RFC 6962](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc6962), [RFC 9162](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc9162)), with Trillian, Sigsum, Rekor and the Go checksum database as working relatives ([github.com](https://github.com/google/trillian), [sigsum.org](https://www.sigsum.org), [sum.golang.org](https://sum.golang.org)). SCITT is the IETF home for exactly this pattern in supply chains ([datatracker.ietf.org](https://datatracker.ietf.org/group/scitt/about/)). Your "ladder that only grows" is this, and nothing more.

**Trusted timestamping.** RFC 3161 is the standard ([datatracker.ietf.org](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc3161)); FreeTSA and OpenTimestamps are the two you use ([freetsa.org](https://freetsa.org), [opentimestamps.org](https://opentimestamps.org)). Bitcoin anchoring is the same idea with a heavier anchor ([bitcoin.org](https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf)), and RFC 4998 defines an Evidence Record Syntax for long-term timestamp validation **[unsure — I did not re-read RFC 4998 to confirm its current status]**.

**Commit-reveal.** Hash commitments, sealed-bid auctions, commit-reveal in smart contracts and time-lock puzzles are all the same primitive ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commitment_scheme), [people.csail.mit.edu](https://people.csail.mit.edu/rivest/pubs/RSW96.pdf)). Your "Möbius strip" is a commitment scheme, and the "seen from the other side" intuition is just binding plus hiding.

**Secret sharing.** Shamir's threshold scheme is the standard reference ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shamir%27s_secret_sharing)); verifiable secret sharing adds exactly the property you want — proof that a share is valid without revealing the secret.

**Pre-registration.** OSF, AsPredicted, Registered Reports and ClinicalTrials.gov are the social version of sealing a prediction in advance ([cos.io](https://www.cos.io/initiatives/registered-reports)); blind analysis in physics is the same move.

**Claim + evidence with a recomputable verdict.** This is the crowded part. The `evidence.*` draft defines append-only, hash-chained, signature-bound records of agent actions, with a tri-state verdict — VALID, INVALID, UNVERIFIABLE — and it is explicit that records are operator-signed and that external witnessing is a deployment measure, not a property of the family [datatracker.ietf.org](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/draft-msebenzi-evidence-action-00). Acta types contributions and attaches burdens to them [github.com](https://github.com/VeritasActa/acta), with state recomputed from the response graph rather than stored [veritasacta.com](https://veritasacta.com/docs). Proof packets derive the verdict at read time rather than trusting the writer [harperz9.github.io](https://harperz9.github.io/papers/proof-packets.pdf). Your "shown / contradicted / not shown" is the same tri-state move, and three-valued logic has a long history ([en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-valued_logic)).

**What is *not* distinct.** Every individual piece. Append-only logs, hash commitments, timestamping, secret sharing, preregistration, and claim-plus-evidence records with recomputable verdicts all exist and are deployed. "Digital time travel" is a metaphor, not a mechanism.

**What might be distinct.** Three things, all **[unsure]** because I could not find a deployed system doing them together. (a) Treating the evidence commitment and the secret share as the *same* record type on one ladder, so that a reveal is itself an auditable entry. (b) Sealing not just a prediction but its resolution rule and its evidence source, then resolving with a machine-computable tri-state verdict. (c) Applying the whole thing inside a live agent loop, where receipts are re-checked in bulk at session start and the agent gets a staleness diff — the belief-ledger note says plainly that the numbers for this don't exist yet and proposes the testbed [ai.bluecloudcyber.com](https://ai.bluecloudcyber.com/notes/belief-ledger). If that is your contribution, it is an empirical and engineering contribution, not a new primitive. Say so.

## 3. Failure modes

**Privacy.** Low-entropy commitments are brute-forceable: a hash of "yes" or of a four-digit code is guessable, so sealed material must be salted. A permanent ladder conflicts with data-minimisation and erasure rights ([gdpr-info.eu](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-17-gdpr/)), and the metadata — timing, frequency, size, who sealed what — leaks even when contents don't. An agent's ledger leaks its users' file paths, internal hostnames and prompts. Revealing a share to prove honesty can reveal the secret.

**Sealed is not true.** A commitment binds you to a claim, not to its truth; a timestamped lie looks *more* credible than an untimestamped one. The evidence itself can be fabricated before sealing, and an adversary who controls the evidence source can manufacture "not shown" or "contradicted" verdicts. "Not shown" is not "false" — absence of evidence is not disproof. And the oracle problem doesn't go away: if a human decides "shown", you are back to trust; if a program decides, you inherit its bugs. The `evidence.*` draft is admirably blunt that a record proves commitment, not correctness, safety, model identity, or independence from the signer [datatracker.ietf.org](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/draft-msebenzi-evidence-action-00).

**Selective reveal is the biggest gaming vector.** If reveal is voluntary, you can seal fifty predictions and reveal the three that landed — survivorship bias with extra steps and a cryptographic alibi. Mitigations: escrowed reveal keys, bonded stakes with slashing, mandatory-reveal registries, or a rule that a sealed entry is void unless revealed by a pre-committed deadline. Without one of these, the ladder actively *helps* the dishonest.

**Who controls the record.** Records are operator-signed, so an operator holding every copy can equivocate between two internally consistent histories — a split view. Transparency logs fix this only with monitors and gossip, and the `evidence.*` draft says external witnessing is a deployment measure, not a family property [datatracker.ietf.org](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/draft-msebenzi-evidence-action-00). Your own trust chain has two links to check: FreeTSA is a single TSA, and OpenTimestamps depends on Bitcoin's cost, latency and block-time accuracy. A TSA proves its clock said so; that is a trust assumption, not a proof about the world.

**Lost keys.** Losing a reveal key converts a sealed claim into a permanent "unverifiable" — which is at least the honest verdict. Losing the signing key stops new sealing but doesn't rewrite the past. The dominant long-run failure is not keys but **link rot**: URLs rot, repos are deleted, directories are renamed, so evidence sources die and beliefs become orphaned. Plan for key rotation, threshold signing and social recovery; assume the evidence will outlive its source.

**Misreading a timestamp.** A timestamp proves a document existed *by* a time, not *at* a time, not that you wrote it, not that you were first, and not that it was reviewed. "Blockchain-verified" will be read as "true" by nearly everyone. Also: a pending OpenTimestamps proof is routinely misread as "not timestamped" **[unsure — worth checking how OTS presents pending vs confirmed]**.

**Cost and staleness.** Re-check everything at every session start and the ledger costs as much as re-deriving it; re-check too rarely and it is stale memory with better paperwork. The belief-ledger note names this as an open gap: facts decay at very different rates and there is no published rule for matching check frequency to decay rate [ai.bluecloudcyber.com](https://ai.bluecloudcyber.com/notes/belief-ledger). A related gap is beliefs arriving from another agent: one agent's sealed claim becomes another's evidence, so garbage propagates with a receipt.

**Coercion and legal exposure.** A receipt proves what was committed, not that the committer was free. And a record that never closes is also a record that can always be subpoenaed.

## 4. How to say it

> I build receipts for claims. When an AI agent — or a researcher, or an analyst — says something is done or true, my systems require it to point at evidence that can be re-checked later, and they answer with one of three things: shown, contradicted, or not shown. The record is append-only and hash-chained, and anything I want to commit to before I know the outcome gets sealed first and revealed later, with the seal independently timestamped. Almost none of the cryptography is mine: Merkle logs, hash commitments, RFC 3161 timestamps, Bitcoin anchoring and transparency logs have been standard for years, and claim-plus-evidence records for agents have already been proposed by others. What is genuinely unsettled is the engineering and the evidence: what such a system costs to run, how often it catches a claim that would otherwise have been believed wrongly, and how often it produces a "not shown" that a human then has to adjudicate. So I am careful about what a receipt proves. A timestamp proves a document existed by a certain time. A hash proves bytes didn't change. A sealed prediction proves what I said before I knew the answer. None of that proves the claim is true, that I am honest, or that the record's operator is independent of the claim — and I seal my methods and my falsification thresholds in advance precisely so the record, not my memory, decides what I predicted.

## 5. One experiment

**Sealed pre-registered evaluation of AI systems** — the use I ranked first, chosen over the agent-ledger testbed because the ledger testbed measures your *current* use, not a new one.

*Design.* Recruit 30–60 evaluations of publicly accessible models, run by at least two independent teams. Before any run, seal: the eval items (or their hashes), the scoring script, the model identifier (weight hash or provider-committed version), the resolution rule, the adjudication protocol, the stopping rule, and the analyst's own predictions about outcomes — timestamped with FreeTSA and OpenTimestamps. Then run, publish transcripts, and resolve every claim as shown / contradicted / not shown. Include an **adversarial arm** where participants are paid to get their claim scored "shown".

*Measured.* (1) **Adjudication rate** — fraction of claims where the machine verdict was contested and a human had to decide, plus inter-adjudicator disagreement. (2) **Tamper-evidence rate** — fraction of runs where the sealed hashes did not match what was actually run, and whether those were accidental or deliberate. (3) **Cost ratio** — verification overhead against an unsealed baseline. (4) **False-confidence rate** — how often a sealed, resolved claim was later shown to be wrong about the world (sealed ≠ true).

*Falsification — seal these thresholds too.* The approach fails if any of the following holds: more than ~20% of claims need human adjudication, or two independent resolvers disagree on more than ~10% of them (the machine-checkable claim collapses to trust); or the tamper-evidence rate is near zero while post-hoc disputes are common (the seal isn't catching the real failure mode); or the cost ratio exceeds roughly 2× (nobody adopts it); or sealed claims turn out no more accurate than unsealed ones (the benefit is reputational theatre). Any of those results is publishable and useful — which is the point of sealing the falsification rule before you know the answer.

## What I could not verify

I did not confirm the IETF draft's adoption status or whether it has been independently reviewed; whether Acta or the proof-packets work has been deployed or critiqued; whether anyone has publicly deployed the exact combination of sealed predictions, sealed resolution rules and machine-computed tri-state verdicts; the current status of RFC 4998; or the details of FreeTSA's and OpenTimestamps' operational guarantees. All marked above as **[unsure]**.
